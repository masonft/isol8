Try it

Two copies in.
One secret out.

When a producer copies a chorus and pastes it later in the song, the two copies are identical down to the last sample. Anything added on top of only one of them — an ad-lib, a riser, a vocal chop — is the only thing that differs. Subtract one copy from the other, and that's all you hear.

Find the twins

Songs repeat themselves, but most repeats are played or sung again and never line up exactly. What isol8 is after are sections that were copied: the same block of audio pasted in twice by the producer.

It builds a spectral fingerprint of the whole song and compares every moment with every other one, at every possible offset. Copies show up as long diagonal streaks of near-perfect similarity.

Line them up to the sample

The fingerprint finds the copy to within about 50 ms. That's nowhere near close enough. At 44.1 kHz there are 44,100 samples per second and the copy has to land on the exact one.

isol8 cross-correlates the raw audio around the guess, then tries the best few offsets and keeps the one that cancels deepest.

Flip the phase

Inverting the phase turns the copy upside down: every peak becomes a trough. On its own it sounds exactly the same. Played against the original, the two push the speaker in opposite directions at the same instant.

Add them together

Everything the two copies share cancels to silence. Whatever was only in one of them survives — that's the isolated result. In numbers: result = twin − copy.

isol8 measures how deep the cancellation goes (in dB) frame by frame, and keeps the stretch where it holds.

Why one sample matters

Drag the copy out of line. Even a single sample of error leaves the high frequencies behind, and a few samples ruin the cancellation completely. It's also why isol8 never resamples your song: converting the rate would turn a whole-sample offset into a fractional one.

Taking it out instead

Flip the isolated result and lay it back over the twin, and the hidden sound comes out of that section instead. isol8 does this across the whole track when you export Song without this sound: the full song, with that one sound gone.

Over the section itself, twin − (twin − copy) is just the copy, sample for sample — so the result is the song with the twin swapped for its clean sibling.

When it won't work

Played twice

A chorus sung or played again is never identical. Only copy-pasted audio cancels.

Bus processing

A limiter or compressor over the whole mix reacts differently to each copy, so they stop matching.

Lossy files

MP3 and AAC encode each copy a little differently. It still works, but leaves a faint hiss.

Stretched or retuned

If the copy was time-stretched, pitched or moved off the sample grid, it can't line up.

All of this happens in your browser

The song is decoded, scanned and encoded inside the tab. Nothing is uploaded, so the audio never leaves your device.
